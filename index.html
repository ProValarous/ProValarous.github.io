Vision

Many of our hardest problems are problems of working together.

  • What makes agents of unequal ability cooperate, and keep cooperating?
  • When a team shares one objective, whose welfare does it count?
  • Which human ways of sharing carry over to learning agents?

I study these questions with multi-agent reinforcement learning and game theory, so that machines can learn from cooperation rather than around it.

IEEE FIE 2025

Bridging the Gap Between Theoretical and Practical Reinforcement Learning in Undergraduate Education

M. A. Atif and M. S. Shaikh. 2025 IEEE Frontiers in Education Conference (FIE), pp. 1 to 9.

Abstract

This innovative practice category paper presents an innovative framework for teaching Reinforcement Learning (RL) at the undergraduate level. Recognizing the challenges posed by the complex theoretical foundations of the subject and the need for hands-on algorithmic practice, the proposed approach integrates traditional lectures with interactive lab-based learning. Drawing inspiration from effective pedagogical practices in computer science and engineering, the framework engages students through real-time coding exercises using simulated environments such as OpenAI Gymnasium. The effectiveness of this approach is evaluated through student surveys, instructor feedback, and course performance metrics, demonstrating improvements in understanding, debugging, parameter tuning, and model evaluation. Ultimately, the study provides valuable insight into making Reinforcement Learning more accessible and engaging, thereby equipping students with essential problem-solving skills for real-world applications in Artificial Intelligence.

An integrated lecture-and-lab framework on Gymnasium environments, evaluated against student surveys, instructor feedback and course performance. Gains concentrate in debugging, parameter tuning and model evaluation, where theory-first teaching leaves students weakest.

Navigating the Maze Environment in ROS-2: An Experimental Comparison of Global and Local Planners for Dynamic Trajectory Planning in Mobile Robots

S. M. Hussain, M. A. Atif, S. M. Daniyal, L. Ahmed, B. Memon and Q. Pasta. 2024 IEEE International Conference on Robotics and Automation in Industry (ICRAI), pp. 1 to 7.

Abstract

A critical task in the quickly developing field of robotics is efficient path planning in dynamic environments. Within the framework of ROS2 (Robot Operating System 2), this paper gives a thorough comparative analysis of several autonomous mobile robot path planning algorithms, including A*, Dijkstra, Regulated Pure Pursuit (RPP), and Dynamic Window Approach (DWA). This research investigated the effectiveness of merging local and global path planners in a dynamic maze environment using an experimental platform built on top of ROS2. Metrics include path length, deviation from the planned trajectory, and execution time. Obtained results demonstrate the performance of these algorithms highlighting their strengths and limitations while focusing on how some combinations perform better than others in terms of accuracy, planning time, and reliability.

A*, Dijkstra, Regulated Pure Pursuit and the Dynamic Window Approach paired as global and local planners in a dynamic maze. Which pairing wins depends on the metric, so the two choices cannot be made independently. My contribution: designed and ran the comparison and its ROS 2 harness; lead author of the public code, rosbag recordings and plots.

AAMAS 2027

Does Centralized Learning Help Embodied Agents Coordinate, and When Can a Tabular Study Tell?

M. A. Atif, N. N. Haji, M. E. Atif and M. S. Shaikh.

Abstract

Tabular studies are a common way to compare independent and centralized multi-agent learners, because they avoid function approximation. A natural expectation is that centralized learning, which learns joint actions, helps agents coordinate, especially when their bodies make solo success hard. Earlier work tests this only in parts: it measures coordination for one learning method, compares methods with the agents' bodies fixed, or varies speed but reports only how well the team did. We test it in a predator-prey environment, where teammates cooperatively hunt together while two teams compete. We compare independent Q-learning (IQL) with central Q-learning (CQL) in all four predator and prey pairings, under limits on speed and stamina, on a 5x5 and an 8x8 grid trained for 200,000 episodes each, and check that the agents learn on both. We measure coordination directly, with predictions for the main measurement fixed in advance. In this task, where one predator can capture prey alone, we find three things. First, CQL costs its agents in both roles on both grids, and its predators coordinate no more than IQL's. Second, its one benefit comes from acting on the best joint action: this cut capture time by 42% on the small grid with faster prey and unlimited stamina, but helped nowhere on the larger grid, where the best joint action had mostly never been tried. Third, on the larger grid a tie-breaking default decided which method won, even though the agents had passed a learning check. A tabular comparison can therefore be trusted only if it also checks how much of the state space the agents covered and implements the methods alike. We propose simple checks for both.

With coordination measured directly, centralized Q-learning cost its agents in both roles on both grids, and its predators coordinated no more than independent learners. A tabular comparison can be trusted only if it also checks state-space coverage and implements the methods alike.

arXiv 2026

A Controlled Study of Double DQN and Dueling DQN Under Cross-Environment Transfer

A. Nasir, F. Dossa, M. A. Atif and M. S. Shaikh. Reviewed at IJCNN 2026; revised version in preparation for Transactions on Machine Learning Research (TMLR).

Abstract

Transfer learning in deep reinforcement learning is often motivated by improved stability and reduced training cost, but it can also fail under substantial domain shift. This paper presents a controlled empirical study examining how architectural differences between Double Deep Q-Networks (DDQN) and Dueling DQN influence transfer behavior across environments. Using CartPole as a source task and LunarLander as a structurally distinct target task, we evaluate a fixed layer-wise representation transfer protocol under identical hyperparameters and training conditions, with baseline agents trained from scratch used to contextualize transfer effects. Empirical results show that DDQN consistently avoids negative transfer under the examined setup and maintains learning dynamics comparable to baseline performance in the target environment. In contrast, Dueling DQN consistently exhibits negative transfer under identical conditions, characterized by degraded rewards and unstable optimization behavior. Statistical analysis across multiple random seeds confirms a significant performance gap under transfer. These findings suggest that architectural inductive bias is strongly associated with robustness to cross-environment transfer in value-based deep reinforcement learning under the examined transfer protocol.

CartPole to LunarLander under one fixed transfer protocol. DDQN avoids negative transfer; Dueling DQN shows it consistently. My contribution: supervised the study end to end and edited the manuscript. The two lead authors are undergraduates I trained.

PPAGE: Predator-Prey Archetypal Gridworld Environment

Open-source testbed for controlled study of coordination, equilibrium selection and generalisation in multi-agent reinforcement learning, and the testbed the AAMAS study runs on.

  • A protected physics core behind typed plugins: rewards, observations and actions are swappable registries, so changing the objective under study is a configuration change and not a rewrite.
  • Eight example learning baselines behind a single two-method interface, from tabular Q-learning to asynchronous actor-critic, including a game-theoretic learner.
  • Reproducible by construction: one fixed seed reproduces world construction and rollouts bit for bit.
  • Apache 2.0, Python 3.10 to 3.12, six named software authors, documented install, quickstart and API reference.

About this design

The star behind my name is the khātam, an eight-pointed star made of two interlaced squares. Geometers have drawn it for centuries with nothing but a compass and a straightedge. Here each of its rings has its own colour, and the star only appears where they meet. The five threads under my name work the same way: they start apart and run into a single line.

The pattern behind the page is a clockwork. Every tile holds gears of different sizes. The small inner stars tick quickly. When they complete a turn, the larger stars move, and their lines let go of their neighbours for a moment before meeting them again. When those complete a turn, the large medallions move. Each part keeps its own shape and its own pace, and the pattern holds together because they keep time with one another.

No single moment shows the whole design. A glance catches a few stars ticking. A minute shows a wave of lines parting and rejoining. The design unfolds as the mechanism runs, and it rewards the visitor who stays.

Each piece of work has a door beside it. Approach one and it lets out light, and the light shows what lies behind the title.

Together it is a parable for the question I work on. The parts differ in size, speed and form, the way agents and people differ in strength and constraint. Each has its own limits, and some of their moves pull against one another. The pattern stays whole when they coordinate and when no part is sacrificed to keep it turning. How agents with different capabilities can cooperate in a way that is fair to each, and that lasts, is the problem behind this site.
